% Abstract of the paper
\begin{abstract}
The increasing number of JWST-JADES Ly$\alpha$ star-forming galaxy candidates, considered to be those driving early cosmic reionisation, are limited to low-resolution rest-frame UV and visible observations; their dwarf [OIII]-extreme line emission (EELG) counterparts in the nearby Universe therefore provide important spectral analogues. These EELGs, however, present challenges to photometric selection due to their faint stellar continuum: wide-band photometric surveys are therefore challenged by filter selection biases on their redshift distributions. We instead present Integral Field (IFU) Spectroscopy as a direct, blind selection method with VLT-MUSE, identifying a sample of 38 [OIII] EELGs (green peas) from a selection of 183 ELG-candidates in the fields of massive clusters and producing an lower-bound volume density $(0.15 \pm 0.02) \times 10^{-2}\;$Mpc$^{-3}$ for green peas on $0.15<z<0.86$, three orders of magnitude larger than previous photometric estimates; our identified EELGs exhibit metallicities as low as $12+\log_{10}($O/H$)=7.52\pm0.03$ and equivalent widths as large as $7200\pm300\;$~\AA. We present a source extraction pipeline and spectral fitting to infer the astrophysical properties of these galaxies, including obtaining metallicities and using legacy Spitzer imaging to investigate their broadband IR emission. We further investigate the population distributions of these galaxies and compare to the MUSE flat-field, and new southern-sky populations of these galaxies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
One key ongoing question in extragalactic astronomy involves understanding the process under which the intergalactic medium transitioned from opaque neutral hydrogen to the current transparent, ionised state: the epoch of reionisation (EoR). It is accepted \citep{brunker_2020} that the ionising  radiation of this period ($z>6$) originated from both star forming galaxies (SFGs; eg \cite{robertson2020,tacchella_2025}) and active galactic nuclei (AGN; eg \cite{2004ApJ...604..484M, Madau2014}); with the increasing number of highly star-forming galaxy candidates at redshifts above $z=10$ discovered by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) \citep{Rhoads, hainline_2023} it has become valuable to study their nearby counterparts, from which a more detailed picture can be built of their spectra. 

It is now believed the strongest contribution to ionising radiation by SFGs is from extreme emission line galaxies (EELGs), a class of dwarf galaxies dominated by recent intense starburst, forming massive hot stars with ultraviolet (UV) dominant emission \citep{tang2023, davis2024}. These strongly ionise the surrounding interstellar medium (ISM), causing fluorescence at specific atomic transitions, a process initially established by \cite{menzel}. These extreme lines dominate the galaxies’ spectra, given the low-mass galaxies lack mature stellar populations to contribute to the spectral continuum, and therefore give the observed atomic transition lines extreme equivalent widths (EWs; the fraction of integrated flux on a specific emission line to the continuum level); this mechanism of starburst naturally produces large amounts of Lyman continuum (UV) radiation from the rapidly growing population of young, massive hot stars \citep{madau1999, gigure2008}. It should be noted that this theory of reionisation is complicated by the large neutral hydrogen column densities surrounding star forming nebulae in these young galaxies; \cite{jaskotoey} and \cite{izotov_2018} highlight that for low-mass galaxies a fully-ionised ISM or one with optically thin tunnels allow for the escape of UV radiation into the intergalactic medium. Recent observations of $z>6$ galaxies have established strong correlation between [OIII]-H$\alpha$ emission  and Lyman Continuum (UV) escape \citep{tang2023} (established initially at low $z$ by \cite{flury2022}), and similarly with UV luminosity \citep{endsley2024}: in contrast with earlier \citep{hackman1995} beliefs about starburst, deep-field astronomy has proven EELGs as direct probes of the reionising inter-galactic medium (IGM). 

These EELGs are distinguished by their dominant emission features, with the primary diagnostic depending on the galaxy's redshift. For star-forming galaxies in the early universe these are rest-frame ultraviolet features: a prominent Lyman alpha emission line or break leads to ‘Ly-$\alpha$-’ or ‘Ly-break-’ galaxy classification \citep{Tacchella_2023_2}. Conversely, EELGs in the $z < 1$ universe are discerned by the strength of their [OIII] emission, a feature that is intrinsically visible and dominates their observed optical spectra: those nearby were discovered as a class by GalaxyZoo \citep{cardamone} identification from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS), earning the name ‘green peas’ (GPs) due to their unresolved, [OIII]-extreme imaging causing them to appear green when artificially coloured. 

As JWST-JADES has recently began to discover more SFGs deep into the $z>10$ EoR \citep{hainline_2023}, these GPs have become popular analogues of their high-redshift counterparts (e.g. \cite{brunker_2020, izotov_2018, Rhoads}): they too are low metallicity \citep{jiang}, high specific star forming rate (sSFR) \citep{brunker_2020, liu2022}, and of which a significant number ($\sim~50\%$) of the most ionising show high Ly$\alpha$ escape fraction \citep{jaskotoey,yang_2017}. Detection of Lyman continuum leakage is rare at local redshifts ($z<<1$) and most of these local continuum-leaking galaxies are GPs \citep{yang_2016, izotov_2016}; those of the highest escape fraction are all green peas. This population of galaxies has therefore become a useful analogue of the SFG reionisation mechanism, and has therefore seen the recent development of large pea catalogues \citep{samonski2025}. More recently, these galaxies are often further classified into those with additional strong local H$\alpha$ emission \citep{izotov2011} – ‘Purple Grapes’ – and extremely low-mass, very local $z<0.1$ ‘Blueberry’ Galaxies \citep{yang2017,liu2022}. For the purposes of this study we use the generalised pea description; that of unresolved dwarfs with EW[OIII]$>100\,$\AA$\;$ in the nearby ($z<1$) universe.

We identify [OIII]-extreme (‘pea-like’) galaxies from massive cluster observations by the Multi-Unit Spectroscopic Explorer (MUSE) on the Very Large Telescope \citep{bacon2010}. This optical Integral Field Unit (IFU) instrument has a 1-arcminute$^2$ field of view on $[4650, 9300]$ nm; this observation band makes the instrument an ideal choice for [OIII] selection on $z<0.86$. These observations of galaxy clusters, the most massive gravitationally bound objects in the observable Universe, are part of the larger VLT-MUSE \textsc{Kaleidoscopes} survey of the most X-ray luminous cluster cores, comparing strong lensing mass models to physical observations \citep{jauzac,jauzac2021,cerny2025}. This task is suited ideally to VLT-MUSE, which can detect galaxies to $z=6$ \citep{bacon2015}, and in this investigation allows for the identification of populations of foreground- cluster member- and background galaxies. Notably, because of their high mass, these clusters magnify background sources through gravitational lensing; this has become a powerful tool to map dark matter distributions in clusters \citep{massey2010}, and can be used to magnify faint background galaxies at high $z$ (prior to JWST this was the leading strategy for direct high-z detection using Hubble; see \cite{livermore2017, bouwens2018,kawamata2018} for prominent examples).

EELG-like objects are often difficult to find uniformly with a traditional photometric prior-informed finding procedure: their faint stellar continuum often renders them invisible in photometric bands that do not align with specific emission lines and therefore creates a selection bias around survey photometric filters. Recent efforts to instead use deeper \textit{random look} spectroscopy provide opportunities to establish estimates of the GP volume density \citep{amorin2015, brunker_2020, miller2024}, but are limited in census area, while SED-fitting-led wide-area methods instead are limited by a lack of spectroscopic follow-up \cite{yang2017, samonski2025}, and induce significant uncertainty in their stellar population models without spectroscopic fits of emission lines. 

In this paper we pose integral field spectroscopy (IFS) as a possible solution to these challenges. The technique has seen very limited use before on individual GP observation \citep{bosch2019, polonio2023}, although it lacks any GP survey application. With the nearby advent of ELT-HARMONI, MUSE’s next-generation successor on the Extremely Large Telescope \citep{harmoni2021}, we showcase IFS as a key tool for extreme emission identification. From our observations we identify a representative sample of EELGs, investigating their physical properties and comparing our populations with KISS and SDSS populations \citep{brunker_2020,samonski2025, jiang}.

\textbf{Section~\ref{sec:obs}: Observations} outlines observational data and the source extraction pipeline used on candidate EELGs, including using corresponding HST photometry and a wide-field survey for population comparisons. We then outline population selection including AGN contaminant rejection and line ratio tools to identify green peas from the parent sample of candidates in \textbf{Section~\ref{sec:pop}: EELG Identification}. From these identified peas, we investigate astrophysical properties in \textbf{Section~\ref{sec:ast}: Physical Properties}, including investigations on SFR, mass and metallicity, and an IR view of the galaxies. Finally, we discuss extremes of this population and survey limits in comparison to contemporary work in \textbf{Section~\ref{sec:dis}: Discussion}, concluding and outlining future work, including links to modern spectroscopy and integral field surveys in \textbf{Section~\ref{sec:conc}: Conclusion}.

Distance-dependent quantities in this paper assume flat $\Lambda$CDM cosmology consistent with \cite{planck2020}; $H_0=66.66$ km s$^{-1}$ and $\Omega_m=0.3097$.

Between November 2017 and January 2018 VLT-MUSE \citep{bacon2010} observed over 30 hours\footnote{I will include an observations table in the final write-up of my project} of the cores of the most x-ray luminous clusters, constituting the \textsc{Kaleidoscopes} survey (ESO project 0100.A0792(A), PI: Edge). Each cluster observation consists of three sequentially imaged exposures of 970 s each. Each exposure sees a 0.3 arcsecond dither applied to minimise observational systematics, with exposures then stacked into an individual cube. These observations, part of a poor-weather-conditions proposal, are conducted in clear conditions but with widely varying seeing on [1.2,1.7] arcsecond. Each MUSE field covers a $1\times1$ arcmin$^2$ footprint, producing a data cube (once reduced) with spectral range on $[4750,9350]\;$\AA$\;$on a wavelength grid of $1.25 \;$\AA$\;$pixel$^{-1}$. 

Following similar methodology to \cite{fumigalli2017, jauzac, cerny2025}, the data are reduced by the European Southern Observatory (ESO) \texttt{esorex} pipeline \citep{weilbacher2020}, following the MUSE Data Reduction Pipeline User Manual\footnote{https://www.eso.org/sci/software/pipelines/muse/} with additional post-processing described in e.g. \cite{richard2021, lagatutta2022, cerny2025}; for clarity to the reader we summarise these here. The initial phase of the reduction involves the creation of master calibration products from raw exposures. This includes generating master bias and flat-field frames, a trace table to map the slitlet positions on the detector, a precise wavelength solution, and an estimate of the Line Spread Function (LSF) for each observing night. Additional illumination corrections are derived from twilight exposures and night-time internal flat-field calibrations. These calibration products are then applied to the raw science exposures, which are subsequently processed into non-interpolated pixel tables, a format that reduces processing cost without affecting the output product.


At the pixel-table level, flux calibration and telluric correction are performed using observations of standard stars taken with the science frames. The data are corrected for differential atmospheric dispersion, and velocities are transformed to the solar system’s barycentric rest frame. For observations utilising Laser Guide Star Adaptive Optics, residual Raman-line flux is also fitted and subtracted. Each exposure is astrometrically aligned to a common World Coordinate System (WCS), defined by high-resolution ancillary imaging from the Hubble Space Telescope (HST) of the cluster. This alignment is achieved by cross-correlating MUSE data with HST F606W and F814W band imaging, providing a secondary flux normalisation by matching photometry in overlapping filter bands.


Following these corrections, each calibrated pixel table is resampled into a data cube. During this process, a self-calibration routine is employed to correct for low-level ($\sim1\%$) flat-fielding systematics that manifest as a weaving pattern across the field of view. This procedure relies on an accurate measurement of the sky background within each slitlet. In crowded fields, the presence of bright cluster members and extended intra-cluster light (ICL) can severely bias this measurement. A sky mask is therefore generated by thresholding a deep continuum image (from HST or a preliminary MUSE white-light image) to exclude these bright sources from the self-calibration calculation.

Once all individual exposures are processed into aligned, self-calibrated data cubes, they are combined into a final master cube. This combination is performed outside the standard pipeline using the \texttt{mpdaf} package. The process involves calculating an inverse-variance weighted average, incorporating a 3$\sigma$ clipping algorithm to reject remaining cosmic rays and other detector defects. Flux scaling factors, derived either from atmospheric transmission variations or from alignment with HST photometry, are applied to ensure a consistent flux scale across all exposures. The final step in the reduction sequence is the removal of residual sky-subtraction artefacts. This is accomplished using the Zurich Atmospheric Purge (ZAP, \cite{soto2016}) tool, which applies a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to spectra in background regions of the data cube. By identifying and subtracting the principal components associated with sky emission residuals, ZAP significantly improves the quality of the final data product, particularly at longer wavelengths. This procedure also requires an object mask to ensure that the PCA is performed only on regions of clean sky. 

From the \textsc{Kaleidoscopes} survey we select EELG candidates from 32 cluster data cubes; \textbf{Figure \ref{fig:allsky}} documents the all-sky position of these fields, along with the location of the two MUSE-WIDE fields used for population comparison.

\subsubsection{Source Extraction}


For the 32 datacubes processed, we led extraction of extreme line emitters on identifying the strongest visible emission line; for these extreme emitters these were [OIII], [H$\alpha$], [OII] and Ly$\alpha$ lines. For this paper, we aim to extract EELGs with strong visible [OIII] emission, indicating galaxies on local redshift with strongly ionising pea-like properties. For each cube, continuum objects were masked and from the resulting data a manual inspection boxcar search was performed along the wavelength axis~\footnote{This is a very technical description of how I would describe our 'manual source extraction' method - I am aware of how it's done in a couple of the CEA cluster papers where they collapse into 'Narrow Band Photometry' and SExtract from there, and similarly the way MUSEWide use a matched filter for the whole process}. Objects with short, high SNR emission were flagged from this search and a resulting catalogue of emission-line candidates was produced; this left 205 total objects. This population of objects was then reduced into a series of sub-cubes, from which a Gaussian ellipsoid was fitted around the 2 arcsecond radius containing the brightest emission to identify a centroid (\textsc{Sextractor}, \cite{bertin1996}). With this corrected centroid identified, and since all objects are unresolved, the sub-cube was spatially collapsed around a 1.7 arcsecond ellipsoid (the maximum seeing-limited full-width-half-maximum), producing a spectrum on  [4750,9350] $\AA$ binned on a wavelength grid of $1.25\;$ \AA$\;$pixel$^{-1}$. \textbf{Figure \ref{fig:clusterMUSE}} demonstrates an artificial colour MUSE continuum view of example cluster MACS0033-07 with 1D and 2D spectra of both an example EELG and an older cluster-member galaxy identified from the Dark Energy Survey (DES, \cite{desdr1}) Data Release 1 catalogue to illustrate the difference in spectral features.

From these spectra, the [OIII] doublet was identified with a matched filter search along the observed wavelength axis. Once identified, the [OIII]$_{5007}$ line was used to measure the redshifts of the 172 filter-selected sources, and spectra failing the search were manually classified into doublet-containing ([OII]-likely) or doublet-missing (LyA-likely) sources. \textbf{Figure \ref{fig:zdist}} documents the objective and cluster-reference redshift distributions of all sources, including the [OIII] selected emitters, which cover a redshift range $0.15<z<0.86$. This is notably deeper than traditional green pea catalogues, owing to both the spatial bias in the sample from gravitational infall to clusters by nearby members, and the ability to detect [OIII] out to $\sim9000\;$ \AA, in comparison to the photometric filter-selected objects of the SDSS \cite{cardamone, jiang, yang_2016, samonski2025}.

\subsubsection{Spectral Catalogue Creation}
\label{sec:cat}

From this selection of [OIII] containing spectra, we transpose each spectra to rest-frame wavelength conduct individual spectral fitting on the lines identified in Table~\ref{tab:rest_wavelengths} in \textbf{Appendix~\ref{app:lines}}. For non-doublet lines, each line is fit on a $50\;$\AA$\;$ window with a Gaussian profile using \cite{marquardt1963} least-square fitting. For fitting windows containing nearby resolved lines (ie resolved doublets and close lines), all other lines are masked to avoid overestimating the continuum; for unresolved doublets a double Gaussian model was used to fit both lines simultaneously to accurately measure the flux of both lines. In each double-Gaussian case the spectra have suitably precise wavelength resolution to identify each peak, and the $\sim50$ available data points is still suitably large compared to the six degrees-of-freedom double Gaussian model for a reliable fit. \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:peas}} demonstrates the individual rest-frame spectra with corresponding WFC3-F775W band MUSE continuum images, while \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:fitting}} demonstrates example line fitting models for a purple grape in the RXJ0232-44 cluster. Given the orthogonality of these Gaussian parameters, the corresponding fits produce suitably diagonal covariances, which are used to estimate the error on the fit flux, centroid and continuum.

We correct the catalogues of emission line fluxes for dust extinction using Balmer decrement. Assuming these hydrogen lines are emitted from the optically thick H$_{\mathrm{II}}$ regions surrounding the ionised nebulae within these galaxies obeying case B recombination, at temperature $T\approx10^{5}$ K for electron density $n_e\approx10^2$ we take intrinsic ratios $\mathrm{H}\alpha/\mathrm{H}\beta=2.863$ and $\mathrm{H}\gamma/\mathrm{H}\beta=0.406$ \citep{osterbrock}. Adopting the \cite{calzetti} starburst extinction curve, we calculate a nebular colour excess of 
\begin{equation}
E(B-V)_{\mathrm{neb}} =  \frac{ 2\log_{10} [(f_{\mathrm{H}\alpha }/f_{\mathrm{H}\beta})/2.863]}{5[k(\mathrm{H}\beta)-k(\mathrm{H}\alpha)]}
\end{equation}
for sources with visible $\mathrm{H}\alpha$ emission, while we use
\begin{equation}
E(B-V) _{\mathrm{neb}}  =  \frac{ 2\log_{10} [(f_{\mathrm{H}\beta }/f_{\mathrm{H}\gamma })/0.406]}{5[k(\mathrm{H}\gamma)-k(\mathrm{H}\beta)]}
\end{equation}
for those without, of those which have at least an SNR of 5 on their $\mathrm{H}\gamma$ flux, where $k(\mathrm{H}\alpha)=3.33$, $k(\mathrm{H}\beta)=4.60$, and $k(\mathrm{H}\gamma)=5.13$ are the specific extinction curve values. We document the reddening correction distribution in \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:red}}; it follows the dust reddening for these galaxies is small, with median correction 0.286 mag. For galaxies lacking strong detection on their $\mathrm{H}\gamma$ line, we apply the median correction. We similarly plot the [OIII]$_{5007}/$[OIII]$_{4959}$ diagnostic, which follows a tight distribution around the expected ratio of $3$. This produced a catalogue of extinction-corrected fluxes for 172 objects, from which we select EELGs in \textbf{Section~\ref{sec:pop}}.

\subsubsection{MUSE-WIDE}
For discussion on survey depth and population comparisons, we choose the \cite{musewide_description} MUSE-WIDE survey, a VLT-MUSE 3D blind spectroscopic survey of emission line objects in the COSMOS \citep{cosmos} and GOOD-S \citep{goods} fields, highlighted in green as part of \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:allsky}}. The survey provides ideal selection depth comparison for emission line objects, and like this investigation is designed as a blind spectroscopy-first selection of emission sources without photometric preselection \citep{herenz2017}. The MUSE-wide survey also covers a mosaic of $1\times1$ arcmin$^2$ observing fields, in this case an area of 100 of these panels. We utilise their catalogue of emission line objects, which is compiled using the \cite{lsdcat} 3D matched filter integral field source extractor \texttt{LSDCAT}, designed for blind emission line source extraction; we aim to evaluate the biases of our inspection methodology against this blind extraction method. This catalogue does not provide estimates of the equivalent widths of detected lines; we estimate these using the \cite{skelton2017} cross-matched CANDELS photometry of each source by identifying the WFC3 band corresponding to the line detection, subtracting the contained emission line flux from the total photometric flux to generate an estimate of the underlying stellar continuum from which to calculate the equivalent width. For like-for-like survey comparison, we select only from [OIII] detected sources. We provide survey comparison and population discussions in \textbf{Section~\ref{sec:dis}}.

\subsection{Legacy Photometry}
\label{sec:phot}

\subsubsection{Hubble Space Telescope}
Visible and near-infrared (NIR) imaging\footnote{I note that this is included because it will be useful for the SED fitting in my final report; I don't actually use any Hubble imaging for science in this specific paper, but I felt it useful to include the methodology as it made the cluster plot on the following page} is obtained from the \textit{Hubble Space Telescope} (\textit{HST}) archives, querying the Mikulski Archive for Space Telescopes (MAST) portal. For each source in our catalogue, we query available imaging on a 1 arcminute spiral search from the Advanced Camera for Surveys (ACS) and the Wide Field Camera 3 (WFC3) in both the UVIS and IR channels. The specific filters targeted were F435W, F606W, and F814W for ACS/WFC, and F225W, F275W, F125W, and F160W for WFC3/UVIS and WFC3/IR, respectively. We prioritise calibrated, drizzled science frames to ensure the use of geometrically corrected and combined data products. The query criteria required a minimum exposure time of 100 seconds, excluding short or failed observations; for each required band, the first suitable data product returned by the query was used for analysis.

\subsubsection{Spitzer Space Telescope}
Archival infrared imaging is sourced from the \textit{Spitzer Space Telescope} Heritage Archive (SSHA), accessed via the NASA/IPAC Infrared Science Archive (IRSA). We use the Simple Image Access (SIA) protocol to query for available data within a 5 arcminute radius of each source position. Our search targets only Level 2 calibrated data products from the Infrared Array Camera (IRAC) and the Multiband Imaging Photometer for Spitzer (MIPS). Specifically, we retrieve mosaics for IRAC channels 1 and 2 (corresponding to 3.6 and 4.5 $\mu$m) and MIPS channel 1 (24 $\mu$m). The query was filtered to select only `science` designated data products, ensuring the use of final, calibrated mosaics for photometric measurements. Although IRAC observations exist for every GP identified in \textbf{Section~\ref{sec:pop}}, MIPS imaging exists for only two; this prevented use of submillimeter imaging in our science.

\subsubsection{Source Extraction}

\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:HST}} demonstrates the HST-Spitzer view of the A209 cluster, identifying example strong line emitters. We perform photometry using the \texttt{photutils} package, an \texttt{Astropy} affiliated library \citep{photutils}. For each science image, we employ the corresponding WCS information to transform the ICRS celestial coordinates into the corresponding pixel positions.

We employ fixed-aperture photometry for all measurements. A circular aperture with a radius of 2.0 arcseconds, chosen to maximise the signal-to-noise each object, was defined at the Gaussian ellipsoid centre of each source. For HST data, the local sky background was estimated on an object-by-object basis using a circular annulus centred on the source, with an inner radius of 3.0 arcseconds and an outer radius of 5.0 arcseconds; this was the maximum outer radius to prevent background contamination for objects near crowded regions. The flux within this annulus was used to determine the local background level, which was subsequently subtracted from the source aperture flux. Given the lower spatial resolution of Spitzer data, we employ local segmentation background subtraction to prevent contamination using \cite{profound} \texttt{ProFound}-generated local noise maps, from which we then apply a circular 2.0 arcsecond aperture to calculate the object flux. The peas identified are compact with extended profiles when observed with HST.

The final flux values were converted to units of microjanskys ($\mu$Jy). For \textit{HST} data, this conversion was performed using the `PHOTFLAM' and `PHOTPLAM' converters, which define the AB magnitude zero-point. For \textit{Spitzer} data, the conversion from the native units of MJy/sr was achieved using the `FLUXCONV` keyword in conjunction with the pixel area derived from the image WCS. The resulting flux measurements for each source across all available bands were compiled to the image catalogue with those identified in MUSE source extraction.

\section{EELG Identification}
\label{sec:pop}

In order to select extreme emission candidates from our identified population of star-forming [OIII] galaxies, we investigate the spectral properties of the 172 identified ELGs to extract those with extreme characteristics. Prior to the determination of any metallicities or spectral fitting, we utilise the common gas metallicity and ionisation parameters O32 and R23. These two parameters identify the ratio and combination of the [OIII], H$\beta$ and [OII] lines as strong line gas proxies, most useful in candidates lacking auroral [OIII] detection. We use the common definitions
\begin{equation}
R23 = \frac{F_{\mathrm{[OIII]}\lambda5007}+ F_{\mathrm{[OIII]}\lambda4959}+ F_{\mathrm{[OII]}\lambda3729}+ F_{\mathrm{[OII]}\lambda3726}}{ F_{\mathrm{H}\beta} }
\end{equation}
for metallicity tracer R23 and 
\begin{equation}
O32 = \frac{F_{\mathrm{[OIII]}\lambda5007}}{ F_{\mathrm{[OII]}\lambda3729}+ F_{\mathrm{[OII]}\lambda3726}}
\end{equation}
for ionisation tracer O32, propagating errors directly from measured fluxes. Although formulation of O32 differs as to whether the $\mathrm{[OIII]}\lambda4959$ flux is included, we exclude it in our calculations (despite the fact our doublet flux ratio is tightly distributed around 3 and in all identified peas SNR$>10$ on the line); this allows for our spectral tracer results to be comparable for most contemporary strong line literature.

Of the 172 identified strong line candidates, 38 meet the EW[OIII$]>100\;$\AA$\;$ criteria to be considered an extreme emission Line galaxy, of which thirteen have visible H$\alpha$ emission. Of these identified peas, we observe [OIII] flux on $1.05\pm0.05<F [\times10^{-17}$erg/ s cm$^2$$] <60.06\pm0.01$, with maximum equivalent width $7200\pm300\;$\AA. For a redshift range of $0.15<z<0.85$ we retrieve an (uncorrected) green pea lower limit volume density of $(0.15 \pm 0.02) \times 10^{-2}\;$Mpc$^{-3}$, assuming a shot noise dominated measurement. \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:populations}} demonstrates the selected population space for these galaxies with their electron temperature tracer. We note that our much deeper redshift selection than the \cite{cardamone} and \cite{jiang} green peas means that we preferentially sample a higher luminosity population at deeper redshift

We additionally observe the expected increase in [OIII] equivalent width with O32, since galaxies with the most strong starburst (dominated by massive young stars leading to little stellar continuum) would expected to also present as the most ionising. This equivalent width relationship further extends to the R23 turnover, where for the most ionising candidates, we observe the central limit of the extreme equivalent widths below the turnover, indicating low-metallicity stellar populations driving nebular ionisation. We highlight, however, the degeneracy present in O32 to R23 as a nebular emission tracer; further investigation in \textbf{Section~\ref{sec:ast}} instead uses the \cite{izotov_2018} R23$-0.08$O32 calibration instead for more robust comparison.

It should be noted that these four most extreme O32 candidates are likely of strong enough ionisation that they have a significant escape fraction; the work of \cite{izotov_2018} demonstrates a significant correlation between continuum escape and O32. Further UV spectroscopy of these galaxies would allow for an estimate on this escape fraction. \cite{izotov_2018} establishes this specialist class of extreme-ionisation green peas as rare: \cite{jiang} find only 835 in the SDSS (a surface density ($(1.59\pm0.05)\times10^{-5}\;$arcmin$^{-2}$); assuming shot noise dominated measurement we therefore detect a significantly greater surface density ($0.13\pm0.06\;$arcmin$^{-2}$) of these galaxies than traditional photometric selection methods. 

Of the survey-identified ELGs, 81 exhibit both H$\alpha$ and NeII detectable above a SNR threshold of 5, allowing us to plot a Baldwin, Phillips \& Terlevich (BPT, \cite{baldwin1981}) diagram to identify populations of starburst galaxies and AGN. From the ELG population identified, none of the observed AGN candidates had X-ray activitiy observable in legacy CHANDRA imaging; this is likely owing to the low mass and age of these galaxies.

With this population of green pea candidates identified, the following section leads with an analysis of the spectral astrophysical properties. We note that the following analysis lacks spectral-energy-distribution (SED) fitting to determine the stellar mass and star formation history; owing to these objects' lack of UV and submillimeter imaging, we note that stellar population synthesis is likely to show inaccuracies (as identified by \cite{brunker_2020, langeroodi2023}), given optical spectroscopy lacks the dust (IR) and young stellar (UV) information to fully constrain the SFH.

\section{Physical Properties}
\label{sec:ast}

\subsection{Metallicity Determination}
\label{sec:metal}
Given that metallicity has established to correlate mass generally in green peas, and given further that it is an important tracer of the stellar population maturity in a galaxy, we determine the $12+\log_{10}O/H$ gas-phase metallicities of our sample. In order to determine this for each extreme emission line galaxy we use a combination of direct electron temperature (for the 10 galaxies with an observed [OIII] aurora line at a SNR$>5$), and the \cite{langeroodi2024} nonparametric strong line kernel density estimator for those that do not. 

To estimate the auroral electron temperature, we follow the methodology of \cite{izotov2006}, calculating recursively a 
\begin{equation}
t_3=\frac{1.432}{\log_{10}[(\lambda4959+\lambda5006)/\lambda4363]-\log_{10}C_t}
\end{equation}
Where $t_3=10^{-4}T_e(\mathrm{[OIII]})$, for some $C_T$ that follows
\begin{equation}
C_T = (8.44 - 1.09t_3 + 0.5t_3^2-0.08t_3^3)
\end{equation}
noting that as established in \cite{izotov2006} the N$_e$(SII) density is discarded given its size. From here we estimate $O^+$ electron temperature following \cite{jiang}
\begin{equation}
t_2 = -0.577 + t_3 \times(2.065-0.498t_3)
\end{equation}
for $t_2=10^{-4}T_e(\mathrm{[OII]})$. From this we calculate $O^+$ and $O^{++}$ abundances 
\begin{multline}
    12 + \log \frac{O^{+}}{H^{+}} = \log \frac{[OII]\lambda 3726 + [OII]\lambda 3729}{H\beta} + 5.961 \\+ \frac{1.676}{t_2} - 0.40\log t_2 - 0.034t_2 + \log(1 + 1.35 \times 10^{-4} n_e t_3^{-0.5})
\end{multline}
% \begin{equation}
% 12 + \log \frac{O^{+}}{H^{+}} = \log \frac{[OII]\lambda 3726 + [OII]\lambda 3729}{H\beta} + 5.961 + \frac{1.676}{t_2} - 0.40\log t_2 - 0.034t_2 + \log(1 + 1.35 \times 10^{-4} n_e t_3^{-0.5})
% \end{equation}
% \begin{equation}
% 12 + \log \frac{O^{++}}{H^{+}} = \log \frac{[OIII]\lambda 4959 + [OIII]\lambda 5007}{H\beta} + 6.200 + \frac{1.251}{t_3} - 0.55\log t_3 - 0.014t_3
% \end{equation}
\begin{multline}
    12 + \log \frac{O^{++}}{H^{+}} = \log \frac{[OIII]\lambda 4959 + [OIII]\lambda 5007}{H\beta}\\ + 6.200 + \frac{1.251}{t_3} - 0.55\log t_3 - 0.014t_3
\end{multline}
and combine given $O/H\simeq(O^+ + O^{++})$. We note again that the determination electron density is negligible on this calculation of metallicity, as established in \cite{izotov2006,jiang}. Given the iterative solution method, we propagate errors using a Monte-Carlo propagation, having verified flux errors follow Gaussian distributions about their measured error. Given the comparitively low SNR of the auroral line in these spectra, it follows that the error on this metallicity is dominant in the random error from the [OIII] auroral flux fit. 

Although polynomial calibration of the strong line O32 and R23 metrics is commonly used to estimate gas phase metallicity in green peas (eg \cite{jiang, sarkar2025, maiolino2008,yabe2014}), their metallicity marginalisation onto a 2D parameter space often makes them insensitive to low-metallicity objects, and makes them prone to metallicity ‘hot’ spots, especially given the non-monotonicity of R23 to electron temperature. Additionally, recent parametric calibrations of NIRSpec-identified galaxies at $z>0.5$ \citep{sanders2024, laseter2024, samonski2025} have shown significant deviation from strong line calibration, indicating non-universality of these metrics. Given this uncertainty surrounding polynomial calibration, we instead opt to use the more reliably statistically calibrated non-parametric \cite{langeroodi2024} \texttt{genesis-metallicity}, built on the spectra of 1510 JWST/NIRSpec and SDSS observations, and operating in a 5-dimensional parameter space. This higher dimensional calibration is universal on $z$ and O32, providing a more accurate strong-line estimate on $\log_{10}O32<0.6$ galaxies, unlike contemporary strong line metrics. The measurement uncertainty from this method is propagated using the moment of the $5D$ posterior distribution on the kernel density estimate.

\subsection{Star Formation and Gas Dispersion}
\label{sec:sfr}

\begin{figure*}
 	\includegraphics[width=14cm]{physicals.png}
 	\captionsetup{justification=Justified}
 	\caption{Nebular astrophysics tracers plotted against metallicities determined in \textbf{Section~\ref{sec:metal}}, with discussion of distributions provided in \textbf{Section~\ref{sec:sfr}}}
        \label{fig:physicals}
\end{figure*}

We further investigate spectral astrophysics of these galaxies in \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:physicals}}. Given the observed tight correlation of H$\alpha$ to H$\beta$ flux, we estimate the star formation rate (SFR) using the \cite{kennicutt1998, kennicutt2021} calibration 
\begin{equation}
SFR [M_{\odot}\; \mathrm{yr}^{-1}] = 7.9 \times 10^{-42} \times L(\mathrm{H}\alpha)\; [\mathrm{ergs\;s}^{-1}]
\end{equation}
estimating the luminosity of the $H\alpha$ line using the intrinsic ratio observed in \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:red}}. Overall we observe the characteristically high SFR expected for these galaxies especially considering their low metallicities (and therefore low masses). For very metal poor peas, we observe the expected metallicity-SFR correlation, given the lack of H$_{II}$ regions in lower metallicity galaxies, and given the increased radiative efficiency of higher metallicity gas. This correlation is however disturbed with higher mass due to competing selection effects; this calculation systematically underestimates the SFR with increasing mass. Additionally,  we calculate SFR using the H$\beta$ flux, which even though extinction corrected is likely to still provide an underestimate of the true H$\beta$ flux given the range of metallicities of galaxies in this investigation. The uncertainty on this measurement is dominated by the random error on the H$\beta$ flux measurement; given the high SNR selection of this population we therefore observe small errors on our SFR measurements.

We calculate the [OIII] velocity dispersion based on the full-width-half-maximum fit of the [OIII]$_{\lambda50007}$ line. Given the intense starburst of these galaxies, we do not use this to calculate a virial mass, since the ongoing intense nebular ionisation violates the (gravitationally bound) assumptions of the virial theorem; a true estimate of stellar and nebular mass would be instead performed by flexible stellar population synthesis. We do, however, observe a weak positive correlation between velocity dispersion and metallicity for very metal poor peas. We theorise that this is due to in-situ starburst feedback within the galaxies, where the very short lifetime of the most massive early-starburst stellar populations injects both synthesized metals and simultaneously shockwaves into the surrounding ISM. We expect a competing selection effect, where higher velocity dispersion from intense nebular winds to cause an increasing escape fraction in these low mass galaxies to disrupt the correlation in the sub-solar metallicity population plotted. The uncertainty in these measurements is dominated by the fitting error on the full-width-half-maximum of the H$\beta$ flux; given that the width of this line on a population scale is centrally distributed around $2.0\pm0.8\;$\AA$\;$, the low sampling of this fit causes larger random error on the measurement of the SFR.

We finally plot the metallicity equivalent width relation, where we observe a population-wide lack of correlation, with a weak correlation for very metal poor galaxies. Although we expect the most extreme emission galaxies to be biased towards lower-mass candidates with very young Wolf-Rayet dominated emission, our deep selection field likely preferentially samples higher mass galaxies, causing a competing selection effect. The error in this measurement is dominated by the random error in the continuum estimation (given the extreme fluxes of all measured [OIII] lines), but this is very small, given the large sampling region around the line ($\approx100\;$\AA) and the masking out of other emission lines.


\subsection{IR Emission}

\begin{figure*}
 	\includegraphics[width=16cm]{irac6.png}
 	\captionsetup{justification=Justified}
 	\caption{IR emission versus [OIII] flux and equivalent width for green pea candidates for Spitzer IRCam channels 1 and 2. The upper panel shows proportional channel flux with corresponding metallicities plotted while the lower panel shows individual band fluxes. Note the difference in rate of change of IR flux across bands due to 4.6 micron emission dominated by nebular emission from highly star forming galaxies. Right hand pane shows BPT-style IR colour vs normalised [OIII] emission plot, isolating four dusty torus-candidate green peas.}
        \label{fig:spitzer}
\end{figure*}

Of this population of pea-Like objects, it is important to diagnose the source of the intense optical emission observed. Given that the radiation can come from both soft starburst emission, and hard active galactic nuclei emission, an infrared tracer serves to diagnose whether the emission is multi-wavelength and therefore of AGN origin rather than soft emission that covers only the UVIS-NIR spectrum around the nebular emission lines. Following the Spizer photometry obtained in \textbf{Section~\ref{sec:phot}}, we investigate the IR emission of our pea candidates in \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:spitzer}}.

We firstly observe strong correlation between the [OIII] and IR colour of the objects. peas below an [OIII] flux of $3.1\times10^{-17}$erg / s cm$^2$ show strong linearity between [OIII] and IR colour, while those above show constant IR colour at $F_{4.5\mu}/F_{3.6\mu}\approx2.5$. We observe weaker non-monotonic correlation in the equivalent width, peaking at $\approx400\;$\AA, either side of which the IR colour decreases. In order to identify potential AGN, we construct a BPT-style tracer of a H$\beta$-normalised IR colour against the H$\beta$-normalised [OIII] flux; we identify four objects with large IR colours compared to the rest of the population, which we plot with purple outline on the previous panes. We observe an empirical starburst-IR colour limit at $\log_{10}(F_{4.5\mu}-F_{3.6\mu})/F_{H\beta}<-2$.

This plot reveals starburst evolution and feedback. We first consider the correlation observed between the [OIII] flux and the $F_{4.5\mu}/F_{3.6\mu}$ colour, serving as a proxy for hot dust emission relative to stellar mass. For objects with [OIII] flux below $3.1\times10^{-17}$erg/ s cm$^2$, the linearity suggests a direct coupling where the increasing power of the starburst, traced by [OIII], proportionally heats the surrounding dust. However, the plateau in IR colour at $F_{4.5\mu}/F_{3.6\mu}\approx2.5$ for more powerful objects implies a saturation point in this heating mechanism. This saturation may arise either from the destruction of the smallest dust grains by the increasingly harsh radiation fields of the most intense starbursts, or it may represent an upper limit to the dust heating efficiency achievable by stellar sources alone. 

The second diagnostic, plotting equivalent width against IR colour, provides an evolutionary context. The non-monotonic trend, peaking at an EW of $\approx400\;$\AA, traces the lifecycle of the burst; as the starburst increases, the young stellar population increasingly dominates and heats the dust, raising the IR colour. For the most extreme, youngest starbursts with EW$>400\;$\AA, the IR colour begins to decrease, suggesting that the feedback from these young stellar populations is already clearing the surrounding interstellar medium, either by destroying the dust or by evacuating it from the environment of the hot stars, reducing the efficiency of dust heating. 

Finally, our BPT-style empirical plane separates objects based on their dust heating relative to their gaseous ionisation. The four identified outliers exhibit an excess of hot dust emission inconsistent with the level of ionisation from star formation, a classic signature of a central Active Galactic Nucleus whose harder, more intense radiation field heats a compact dusty torus, also consistent with the otherwise lack of H$\beta$ broadening in their spectra. By removing these candidate AGN, we re-examine the diagnostic plots. This cleans the observed relations; the plateau in the [OIII]-IR colour diagram, becomes more sharply defined, which was otherwise underestimated with the AGN. Similarly, the exclusion of these objects reduces the scatter on the EW-IR colour plot, particularly since AGN continuum emission can artificially lower the EW.

To probe the physics of this evolutionary sequence more deeply, a spectral stacking analysis would be highly valuable. By co-adding the spectra of galaxies binned by their position on the EW-IR colour diagramm, creating composite spectra for the \textit{rising}, \textit{peak}, and \textit{falling} populations, we could achieve a signal-to-noise ratio sufficient to detect more subtle diagnostic features. We would predict that the stacked spectrum of the \textit{falling} population (EW > 400 Å) might reveal broad underlying components or asymmetric blue-wings in the emission lines, providing confirmation of the powerful outflows that we infer are clearing the ISM. Furthermore, faint Wolf-Rayet stellar features should be most prominent in the \textit{peak} and \textit{falling} stacks, directly confirming the age sequence we have proposed.


\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:dis}

\subsection{Population Comparisons}
\label{sec:rep}

\begin{figure}
 	\includegraphics[width=8cm]{discussion.png}
 	\captionsetup{justification=Justified}
 	\caption{Population line ratio comparisons between this work and MUSE-WIDE survey results. Each pane weighted to survey area between the two populations. All metallicities for MUSE-wide calculated using \cite{langeroodi2024} KDE; note that the increased sensitivity of the MUSE-WIDE survey allows for preferential sampling of very low metallicity galaxies.}
        \label{fig:discussion}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:discussion}} illustrates weighted population comparisons against the wide-field MUSE-WIDE survey, covering 100 arcmin$^{2}$ of sky region, and a comoving volume of $79513$ Mpc$^{3}$. We succeed in observing preferentially higher equivalent widths for both [OIII] and H$\beta$, establishing this extraction as an effective tool for finding the most extreme emission line galaxies. We note that we also observe higher maximum OIII equivalent widths than MUSE-WIDE; this may likely be due to the lensing amplication of sources from the clusters. We observe similar R23 and O32 distributions, with O32 preferentially skewed towards higher ionisation sources, and R23 largely uniformly matching MUSE-WIDE. Given that green peas exist across a range of R23, this is unsurprising, and verifies that both surveys observe similar populations of extreme emission line galaxies. This work also observes a proportionally larger number of  mid-ionisation sources compared to the wider survey, which skews the strong line metallicity distribution to higher metallicity than the parent distribution, although we establish in \textbf{Figure:~\ref{fig:mcities}} that this is only a reliable calibration of metallicites for the most metal-poor extreme emission line galaxies.

Direct inference of the metallicities produces the most prominent result, however. Where we observe a similar population of low-mid metallicity sources to the parent population, the parent population extracts a distribution of very low metallicity galaxies not observed in this work; this is likely due to the much lower flux limit of MUSE-WIDE's automatic matched filter source extraction. We do however extract a much larger population at $7.5<\log(O/H)<8$ implying that if this measurement is flux limited (since the lowest mass galaxies are those of lowest metallicities, and therefore of the smallest flux) then our extraction would see much more successful extraction of candidates at very low metallicites. Conversely, however, the cluster environment of many of these galaxies may provide metal enrichment (or spectral contamination) and skew the population to higher metallicities; only an investigation of the deeper lensed Lyman-$\alpha$ galaxies would provide confirmation as to which of these is plausible.

The hypothesis that lensing magnification boosts the observed maximum equivalent widths can be directly tested by correlating the [OIII] EW of each source with its magnification factor, as derived from a cluster lensing model. A positive correlation would provide strong evidence that this survey's unique sightlines are responsible for revealing green peas. To distinguish between selection bias and environmental enrichment as the cause for the observed metallicity distribution, we should further investigate the mass-metallicity relation by using flexible population synthesis modelling. If our sample follows the same mass-metallicity as field galaxies but is biased towards higher stellar masses, a flux-limited selection is the likely cause. Conversely, if our galaxies are systematically metal-rich for their mass, it would strongly support the environmental enrichment hypothesis. This can be complemented by a spatial analysis within the clusters; a negative metallicity gradient towards the cluster core would serve as a clear indicator of environmental influence. Finally, by incorporating SED-fit specific star formation rates, we might place our sample in the context of the fundamental metallicity relation, providing a more complete picture of whether these galaxies are enriched due to suppressed star formation or are simply a mass-selected subset of typical star-forming populations.

We observe further a similar volume-corrected R23 and O32 distribution to the SDSS-selected \cite{jiang, yang_2016} green peas, and further similar SFRs to the SDSS \cite{cardamone} and KISS \cite{brunker_2020} green peas; this is significant given that our redshift distribution extends far deeper than both surveys (out to $z<0.86$) and our differing blind spectroscopic selection criteria. This observation supports the conclusion that our observed EELGs are of the same galaxy distribution as other green pea surveys. We reiterate that the much larger redshift selection criteria will see preferential selection of higher luminosity at increasing redshift; we may therefore miss more extreme equivalent widths at higher redshift, given that the most extreme candidates are of very low mass and stellar continuum. Without SED modelling, it is not possible to further classify whether any of these galaxies fall into less extreme luminous blue dwarf or extremely low mass blueberry EELGs, and we note that while GPs are nominally called dwarf galaxies, they possess stellar masses spanning $10^{8.5}<M\;[M_{\odot}]<10^{10.5}$ \citep{brunker_2020}, crossing the full extent of low-intermediate masses. We are therefore limited in further population comparison without stellar population modelling.

\subsection{Survey Completeness and Volume Density}
\label{sec:comp}

\begin{figure}
 	\includegraphics[width=7cm]{lognlogs.png}
 	\captionsetup{justification=Justified}
 	\caption{Log N - Log S plot for [OIII] equivalent with to estimate completeness for this work compared to MUSE-WIDE. As expected, our flux limit produces a greater number of mid-EW detections given the most luminous sources exhibit intermediate EW$<300$\AA}
        \label{fig:lognlogs}
\end{figure}

In order to estimate completeness of this survey we compare to the MUSE-WIDE wide-field catalogue, plotting [OIII] flux and equivalent width logN-logS plots in \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:lognlogs}}. As expected, our increased sensitivity to high-flux sources causes a bulge in intermediate equivalent width (higher mass) green peas, as compared to the parent sample. Following our manual extraction method, we demonstrate a completeness of $\sim20\%$. Similarly, our flux depth limitations see us less sensitive to smaller fluxes. Overall, we observe this method as incomplete as compared to the MUSE-WIDE survey, despite the fact we observe similar representativeness, although we stress that this survey does extend to far more local redshifts than ours, and therefore is likely to include a larger population of low-mass faint blueberry galaxies that exhibit similar extreme equivalent widths.  

As a result, with more complete source extraction we estimate the surface density of the green peas in VLT-MUSE observations to be as much as $\sim5$ peas per square arcminute, and a volume density as large as $\sim 0.75\times 10^{-2}\;$Mpc$^{-3}$, an estimate three orders of magnitude larger than the KISSR GP \cite{brunker_2020} galaxies and one even larger than the preliminary density of these galaxies calculated from objective prism spectroscopy by \cite{salzer2009}.  Although this significantly higher volume density, if confirmed, has implications for our understanding of Lyman continuum escape in the local universe as this source extraction method is likely to increase the population of identified UV-active starburst galaxies in the nearby universe, we note however that there may be inflation due to selection effects. Given that the distribution of peas is expected to be slightly larger between $0.6<z<0.8$ than on $0<z<0.4$, our uniform selection method over this range (as compared to traditional photometric selection on $0.15<z<0.4$)  may sample an increased density of these galaxies at earlier times closer to cosmic noon. Regardless, this estimate establishes IFU spectroscopy as a highly efficient method for blind GP extraction.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conc}

We have identified a population of 38 new green pea candidates in the fields of 32 massive clusters as observed by VLT-MUSE in the \textsc{Kaleidoscopes} survey. These EELGs exhibit the characteristic extreme soft ionisation typical of intense, early-life starburst, with extreme line emission and a faint stellar continuum. This manifests as low-metallicity stellar populations highly ionising surrounding nebula; the most extreme nebular emission of this class is posed to significantly contribute to the budget of UV photons responsible for re-ionisation of the inter galactic medium up to $z=6$; five of our identified galaxies demonstrate O$^{++}$ ionisation indicative of an escape fraction $f_{esc}>0.5$. We have analysed the astrophysical properties of these peas, observing the characteristic low metallicities and extreme star formation expected from these galaxies, and identified four possible active galactic nuclei from IR investigation of these galaxies.  We pose this investigation as a reliable blind source extraction method for such peas, just as demonstrated for identifying ELGs by the \cite{musewide_description} MUSE-WIDE survey. With the advent of the ELT-HARMONI instrument in the near future, we pose this as a useful source extraction technique for developing deep, blind catalogues of green peas for comparison with the emerging populations of  $z>10$ Lyman-break galaxies identified by the JWST-JADES survey.

We identify three main fields of investigation for future science. Firstly, the mass-metallicity relationship of these galaxies is of interest since the evolution track has been demonstrated to differ depending on the redshifts of the populations; green pea investigations currently lack a determined mass-metallicity track between $0.3<z<0.8$, and this population could establish the relationship for these galaxies. For this, we require a nebular-inclusive SED fitting pipeline, and an investigation would likely combine HST, JWST and Spitzer imaging of these galaxies with a prior developed using the observed spectroscopic emission lines to accurately constrain the mass of the galaxies. Given the star-formation history (SFH) of these galaxies is poorly understood, we recommend use of a stellar emulator with non-parametric SFH; given the new strength of flexible-stellar-population-synthesis interfaces like \textsc{Prospector}, we propose use of a Bayesian nested approach using our collected multi-wavelength data to constrain the mass and ionisation parameter of these galaxies. 

This SED fitting approach, combined with any HSC legacy information and the non-parametric methods used for metallicity inference in this investigation would secondly allow future investigation to estimate the ionising escape fraction for the most extreme O32 candidates in this investigation. This would establish this method as a reliable density estimator for the rare Lyman-leaking green peas observable at $z<1$, which is especially pertinent with the increased sensitivity of future IFU instruments like ELT-HARMONI. With this data, a further deep-field estimate of the volume density of continuum-leaking peas could be calculated, which would be usefully compared against the contemporary \cite{izotov_2016} SDSS population. 

Finally, this paper lacks full investigation into the lensed galaxies detected within the clusters as described by \textbf{Figure~\ref{fig:zdist}}. Future work should carry out spectral investigation on these higher redshift galaxies, and compare the properties of these with the population of green peas identified in this paper and with the much higher redshift Lyman break galaxies identified by JWST; given the power of these cluster lenses, such an investigation should report an overdensity of these objects compared to flat-field estimates, and could further compare to numerical lensing simulations of their parent clusters to investigate observed differences.
